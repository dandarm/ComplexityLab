Final results

Objective and rationale We searched for benchmarks/generators where a univariate time series is synthesized as a sum of sinusoidal terms whose spectral amplitudes follow a power-law (1/f^α), and for evidence that such constructions are used as benchmarks/tests for time-series methods (including forecasting-related evaluation). We summarize formal definitions, practical generation procedures, typical parameterizations, and example uses.

What the benchmark/generator is A standard synthetic process is constructed so that its power spectral density (PSD) follows a power-law S(f) ∝ 1/f^α (pink/red noise family). A concrete time-domain construction is a finite sum of sinusoids with random phases and amplitudes that scale with frequency according to the target spectrum. Equivalently, one may synthesize in the frequency domain by shaping white noise with the desired power-law spectrum and inverse-FFT to the time domain; both are mathematically equivalent to a weighted sum of sinusoids. (1.1)

Formal definition (sum-of-sinusoids form) Let K frequencies {f_k} be chosen in a range [f_min, f_max]. Define amplitudes A_k to match the target spectrum S(f) ∝ 1/f^α via A_k ∝ sqrt(S(f_k)) ∝ f_k^{-α/2}, and draw independent phases φ_k ~ Uniform(0, 2π). Then define x(t) = Σ_{k=1..K} A_k cos(2π f_k t + φ_k), optionally with an additive white-noise term. With sufficiently dense {f_k}, the resulting PSD approaches 1/f^α. This construction follows directly from the Fourier representation and the relation S(f) = |X(f)|^2 (amplitudes are the square-root of the target spectral shape). (1.1)

Equivalent frequency-domain (inverse-FFT) procedure

    Choose a grid of frequencies {f_k} (set by the sampling rate and series length).
    Set |X(f_k)| = c · f_k^{-α/2} for f_k in [f_min, f_max] and |X0| as needed; draw φ_k ~ Uniform(0, 2π); form complex coefficients X(f_k) = |X(f_k)| e^{i φ_k} subject to Hermitian symmetry.
    Inverse FFT to obtain a real-valued x(t). This preserves the target power-law spectrum and is widely used in practice; phases can be randomized because empirical phase spectra often behave like white noise. (2.1, 1.1)

Typical parameters and ranges reported in practice

    Spectral exponent α: studies span α ∈ [−1, 3] for “flicker/colored” noise test signals, with larger α producing steeper red spectra and stronger long-range correlations. (3.1, 3.2)
    Frequencies and K: chosen over the resolvable band implied by sampling (e.g., f_min ≈ 1/T, f_max ≈ Nyquist), with K equal to the number of Fourier modes or a dense subset over [f_min, f_max]. (1.1)
    Phases: φ_k i.i.d. Uniform(0, 2π). (1.1)
    Optional addition of discrete sinusoidal components (e.g., a few low-frequency waves) to study leakage/seasonality effects alongside the power-law background. (4.1)

Evidence of use as benchmarks/tests relevant to forecasting

    Method benchmarking under data limitations: synthetic time series with power-law spectra created as superpositions of sinusoids are used to benchmark spectral-estimation methods and assess bias due to observational gaps, providing a rigorous testbed for algorithms that are also foundational in forecasting pipelines. (4.2, 4.1)
    Practical generation for simulation/forecasting contexts: in wind-energy applications, inverse-FFT synthesis with randomized phases and power-law-shaped amplitudes is used to generate realistic high-frequency wind series for simulation; this approach is directly applicable to stress-testing forecasting models with controlled 1/f^α backgrounds. (2.1, 2.1)
    Statistical learning/estimation benchmarks: flicker-noise (1/f^α) series are used as a benchmark curve to train and evaluate estimators (e.g., ANN predictors of α) and to distinguish stochastic vs. chaotic signals—procedures that can be repurposed to validate forecasting models on controlled long-range dependent signals. (3.2, 3.1)

Caveats and practical notes

    Aliasing and finite-sample effects: estimating α from finite data is biased by sampling/aliasing; design series length and sampling to reduce bias, especially at large α. (4.1)
    Prewhitening/postdarkening: for β = α slopes, prewhitening (differencing) and compensating in frequency can improve estimation stability for steep spectra; these are useful when constructing or validating forecasting pipelines. (4.1)

Minimal recipe (ready to implement)

    Inputs: N (length), Δt (sampling), α, target variance σ^2, optionally add M discrete sinusoids.
    Steps: (i) Define frequency grid f_k = k/(NΔt), k = 1..⌊N/2⌋. (ii) Set amplitudes |X(f_k)| = c · f_k^{-α/2}. (iii) Draw φ_k ~ U[0,2π]; impose Hermitian symmetry. (iv) Inverse FFT to get x(t). (v) Rescale to variance σ^2. (vi) Optionally add Σ_{m=1..M} B_m cos(2π f_m t + ψ_m) to emulate seasonal tones. Supporting theory and usage are documented in the cited works. (1.1, 2.1, 4.1)

Conclusion Time series synthesized as sums of sinusoids with power-law-distributed spectral amplitudes (PSD ∝ 1/f^α) are well-established synthetic benchmarks. They can be generated either explicitly as superpositions with A_k ∝ f_k^{−α/2} and random phases or via inverse-FFT shaping of white noise. These signals are routinely used to benchmark method performance (e.g., spectral estimation under gaps), to train/test estimators of long-range dependence, and in simulation contexts directly relevant to forecasting, and thus are suitable as controlled benchmarks for time-series forecasting models.
